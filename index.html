German Attacks & Operations Targeting New York
World War I & World War II Era

Research package for video essay — Compiled October 2026. Includes key events, historical context, and period photographs. No successful large-scale aerial bombing or direct assault on New York City itself occurred; the main events were harbor sabotage (1916) and failed/near-shore operations in 1942.

1. World War I: Black Tom Explosion (July 30, 1916)

On the night of July 29–30, 1916 (while the United States was still neutral), German agents sabotaged the Black Tom munitions depot in Jersey City, New Jersey — part of New York Harbor. Roughly 2 million pounds of ammunition, shells, and TNT exploded around 2:08 a.m. The blast was powerful enough to register as an earthquake, shatter windows across lower Manhattan, damage the Statue of Liberty (its torch has remained closed to visitors ever since), and be felt for many miles. At least 7 people died; property damage ran into the tens of millions of dollars (1916 value).

Key agents included Kurt Jahnke and Lothar Witzke (with facilitation linked to Michael Kristoff and the broader network of Franz von Rintelen). This was the largest single act in a German sabotage campaign of more than 50 incidents on U.S. soil aimed at disrupting munitions shipments to the Allies. Initial investigations largely dismissed sabotage; later evidence presented to the Mixed Claims Commission established German responsibility. Postwar Germany ultimately paid reparations.

Related WWI Sabotage – Franz von Rintelen

Captain Franz von Rintelen (operating under cover in New York) was a central figure in the wider campaign. He helped develop and deploy “pencil bombs” / cigar-style time-delayed incendiary devices that were planted in ship holds, causing fires at sea and destroying millions of dollars of cargo on dozens of vessels. He also pursued schemes involving powder shortages, labor disruption, and other interference with Allied supply lines from U.S. ports.

2. World War II: Operation Pastorius (June 1942)

The main German sabotage attempt against the continental United States. The Abwehr trained eight agents (several with prior residence in the U.S.; some with German-American Bund or Nazi Party ties) and delivered them by U-boat in rubber rafts. They carried explosives, timers, and substantial cash.

  • One team (George John Dasch, Ernst Peter Burger, Richard Quirin, Heinrich Heinck) landed at Amagansett, Long Island, New York, on the night of 12–13 June 1942 via U-202. They buried supplies and took the train into New York City.
  • The second team landed near Ponte Vedra Beach, Florida, via U-584.

Targets included the Hell Gate Bridge and other New York infrastructure, aluminum plants (including in New York), Niagara Falls hydroelectric facilities, the New York City water system, key railroads, and efforts to create public panic. The plot collapsed almost immediately: Dasch contacted the FBI and confessed, leading to the arrest of all eight men within weeks. They were tried by military commission (Ex parte Quirin). Six were executed; Dasch and Burger received commuted sentences and were later deported.

3. U-Boat Operations off the East Coast – U-123 and Operation Drumbeat (early 1942)

In the opening months of 1942 (Operation Drumbeat / Paukenschlag, the “Second Happy Time”), long-range German U-boats attacked shipping off the poorly defended U.S. East Coast. U-123 under Reinhard Hardegen came especially close to New York. He observed the bright lights of Manhattan and Coney Island (blackout measures were still incomplete), sank ships nearby (including the British tanker Coimbra), and later described the sight of the illuminated city as striking. Hundreds of ships were sunk along the coast before improved convoys and blackouts reduced the threat.

Note: Widely circulated “periscope photos” of the Manhattan skyline from U-123 are generally considered false or composite; Hardegen’s own accounts indicate practical limits on how far into the harbor he went.

4. Unrealized Plans: The Amerikabomber Project

German planners pursued long-range strategic bombers capable of striking New York from Europe (the Amerikabomber program). Contenders included the Messerschmitt Me 264 (first flight of the V1 prototype in December 1942) and the Junkers Ju 390. A few prototypes were built and flown, but none became operational due to range shortfalls, production priorities, and resource constraints. Ideas involving one-way missions, staging via Norway or Greenland, or mid-air refueling remained on paper or in limited testing. Hitler and senior figures expressed interest in seeing New York in flames; U.S. media published speculative illustrations. No such raids occurred.

5. Late-War Intelligence: Operation Teardrop (1945)

In the final months of the war, U.S. Navy forces conducted Operation Teardrop against U-boats in the North Atlantic amid intelligence (later shown to be unfounded) that some submarines were modified to launch V-1 flying bombs at New York and other East Coast cities. Several U-boats were sunk. Interrogations and technical assessment confirmed no such rocket capability was actually deployed against the United States. German propaganda had amplified the threat.
